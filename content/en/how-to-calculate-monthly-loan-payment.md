Knowing your monthly payment before you sign a loan helps you check that it fits your budget and compare offers fairly. The math looks intimidating, but it comes down to one formula.

## The formula

For a fixed-rate loan with equal monthly payments, the payment is:

**M = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n)**

- **M** is the monthly payment.
- **P** is the amount you borrow (the principal).
- **r** is the monthly interest rate: the annual rate divided by 12, written as a decimal.
- **n** is the total number of monthly payments.

Each payment covers that month's interest plus a slice of the principal. Early payments are mostly interest, and later ones are mostly principal, even though the total payment stays the same.

## A worked example

Suppose you borrow 20,000 at 5.5% a year for 5 years.

1. Monthly rate: r = 0.055 ÷ 12 ≈ 0.004583.
2. Number of payments: n = 5 × 12 = 60.
3. Discount factor: (1 + r)^−60 ≈ 0.760, so 1 − 0.760 ≈ 0.240.
4. Payment: M = 20,000 × 0.004583 ÷ 0.240 ≈ 382.02.

Over 60 months you repay about 22,921, so the interest cost is about 2,921.

## Why the loan term matters

Stretching the same loan over a different term changes both the payment and the total interest:

- **3 years:** about 603.92 a month, with roughly 1,741 in total interest.
- **5 years:** about 382.02 a month, with roughly 2,921 in total interest.
- **7 years:** about 287.40 a month, with roughly 4,142 in total interest.

A longer term lowers the monthly payment but raises the total cost. A shorter term does the opposite. Choose the shortest term whose payment you can comfortably afford.

## What if the rate is 0%?

With no interest the formula is simply P ÷ n. A 20,000 loan over 60 months at 0% costs 333.33 a month.

## What this formula does not include

The result covers principal and interest only. Real loans can add:

- Arrangement or processing fees.
- Insurance sold with the loan.
- Variable interest rates that change over time.

To compare offers fairly, look at the total amount you will repay and the annual percentage rate (APR), which folds in many of these costs.

## Practical tips

- Ask the lender for an amortization schedule, which lists how each payment splits between interest and principal.
- Check whether you can make extra payments without a penalty. Paying a little extra early reduces the interest on the remaining balance.
- Run several scenarios with different amounts and terms before you decide.
