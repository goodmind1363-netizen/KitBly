Many websites show an estimate such as "5 min read" at the top of an article. It is a small detail, yet it helps readers decide whether to start. The calculation behind it is simple.

## The formula

**Reading time (minutes) = number of words ÷ words per minute**

The only judgment call is the reading speed. Researchers have found that adults reading non-fiction silently in English average roughly 230 to 240 words per minute, and many publishers assume a round figure between 200 and 250. Our word counter assumes 220 words per minute and rounds up to the next full minute, which errs on the side of a slightly longer estimate.

## Quick conversions at 220 words per minute

- 500 words: about 3 minutes.
- 1,000 words: about 5 minutes.
- 1,500 words: about 7 minutes.
- 2,000 words: about 10 minutes.

## If you are reading aloud or presenting

Speaking is slower than silent reading. A comfortable speaking pace for talks and presentations is often around 130 to 150 words per minute. At 140 words per minute, a 1,000-word script takes about 7 minutes. If you are preparing a speech with a time limit, divide the number of minutes by the pace to find how many words you can fit.

## Why the estimate is only approximate

Reading speed depends on many things:

- **Difficulty.** Technical or unfamiliar text takes longer than light reading.
- **Language.** Speeds differ between languages and writing systems.
- **Layout.** Long paragraphs, small fonts and busy pages slow readers down.
- **Purpose.** Skimming for facts is much faster than studying.
- **Images and tables.** These add time that a pure word count does not capture.

Treat the number as a guide, not a promise.

## Why show reading time at all?

- It sets expectations, so readers are less likely to leave halfway.
- It helps you plan content, such as keeping a newsletter to a few minutes.
- It makes it easy to compare articles and spot ones that run too long.

## Tips for shorter reading time without losing value

1. Lead with the answer, then add detail for readers who want it.
2. Use headings so people can jump to the section they need.
3. Cut repeated points and long lead-ins.
4. Prefer short sentences and plain words.
